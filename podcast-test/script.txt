LINGUALPHABET LISTENING LESSON
Topic: Why Do People Procrastinate?
CEFR Level: B2
Speakers: Alex, Maya
Target Duration: 5–6 minutes

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Alex: Okay, so — I have a confession to make. I've been meaning to write this lesson plan for, like, three weeks now.

Maya: Three weeks? And you've only just started?

Alex: I know, I know. Every time I sit down to do it, I suddenly remember I need to check something, or I'll think, "I'll do it after lunch," and then... I don't.

Maya: You know there's actually a word for that, right? It's not just laziness.

Alex: Procrastination. I'm aware.

Maya: Right, but most people think it means you're lazy or disorganized. That's kind of a misconception.

Alex: So what is it actually?

Maya: It's more about how you manage emotions than how you manage time. Like, when you keep putting something off, it's usually because the task makes you feel uncomfortable — anxious, bored, frustrated, whatever.

Alex: Huh. So when I avoid writing the lesson plan, I'm not avoiding the work exactly — I'm avoiding the feeling?

Maya: Exactly. And the instant you open up a YouTube video or scroll through your phone instead, that discomfort goes away. Just like that.

Alex: Oh, that's sneaky.

Maya: It is. Your brain gets a quick reward for avoiding the thing, and so it keeps doing it.

Alex: That makes a horrible kind of sense. Because I genuinely feel better the second I decide not to do something.

Maya: Right? And that's the trap. The relief feels great in the moment, but then the deadline gets closer and the anxiety builds up even more.

Alex: The task hasn't gone anywhere. It's just... scarier now.

Maya: Exactly. And I think a lot of people make it worse by telling themselves things like, "I work better under pressure." Which — sometimes that might be true, but often it's just a way to justify putting things off.

Alex: Oh, I've definitely said that. Many times.

Maya: (laughs) We all have. But there's a difference between genuinely needing some time to think something through and just... avoiding it because it feels unpleasant.

Alex: So what makes something feel unpleasant enough to procrastinate on? Like, I don't procrastinate on things I enjoy.

Maya: No, right, because those tasks don't trigger that negative emotion. The things people avoid tend to be things that are either boring, overwhelming, or tied to some kind of self-doubt.

Alex: Self-doubt?

Maya: Like, if you're worried the result won't be good enough — or that people will judge you — that's actually a huge driver of procrastination. It's almost like, if you never finish the thing, you can never fail at it.

Alex: Oh wow. That's... actually a bit too real.

Maya: Yeah. It's kind of a weird form of self-protection.

Alex: Okay, so we've got anxiety, self-doubt, boredom, feeling overwhelmed — and I'd add one more thing to that list.

Maya: Phones?

Alex: Phones. Social media. The whole thing.

Maya: Oh, hundred percent.

Alex: Like, I genuinely think my attention span has gotten worse. I'll sit down to work and within five minutes I'm checking Instagram.

Maya: And it's not even like you consciously decide to do it. It's almost automatic.

Alex: Exactly. And then you look up and twenty minutes have gone by and you've watched nine videos about — I don't know — how bridges are built.

Maya: (laughs) Which is interesting, but not what you were supposed to be doing.

Alex: Not helpful.

Maya: The thing about phones is that they provide the same kind of immediate reward that procrastination does. So if your brain is already wired to seek out that quick relief, having your phone nearby makes it so much harder to resist.

Alex: So it's like — procrastination already exists, and then phones make it ten times easier to act on it.

Maya: Pretty much. Some researchers talk about it as an "intention-action gap" — where you fully intend to do something, but then you never actually start.

Alex: I live in that gap. That gap is my home.

Maya: (laughs) So how do you get out of it?

Alex: That's the question. I've tried setting timers. I've tried to-do lists. I've tried being really mean to myself about it, which — spoiler — doesn't work.

Maya: No, self-criticism actually tends to make procrastination worse. Because it adds more negative emotion around the task.

Alex: Which makes you want to avoid it even more.

Maya: Right. What tends to work better is something called self-compassion, which sounds a bit soft, but basically it means acknowledging that you're struggling without making yourself feel terrible about it.

Alex: Like, "Okay, I didn't do the thing today. That's fine. I'll try again tomorrow."

Maya: Kind of, yeah. But with a plan. The "I'll do it tomorrow" part has to have some structure, or it just becomes another way to put things off.

Alex: So — what kind of structure?

Maya: The main thing that seems to work is making the first step really, really small. Not "write the lesson plan," but "open the document." That's it.

Alex: Just open it?

Maya: Just open it. Because the hardest part is usually starting, not continuing. Once you're actually in it, it often becomes manageable.

Alex: Huh. That's actually a lot less scary than "write the lesson plan."

Maya: That's the point. Lower the barrier to entry.

Alex: Okay, I'm going to try that today. Right after this.

Maya: (dryly) Are you though?

Alex: (laughs) Yes! Okay — probably. Maybe.

Maya: Let's do this. One last thing — I think it's worth saying that everyone procrastinates. It's not some character flaw. It's a really human thing. The goal isn't to eliminate it completely, it's just to not let it take over.

Alex: Right. And being a little kinder to yourself about it probably helps more than beating yourself up.

Maya: Definitely. Okay — lesson plan. Go.

Alex: Going. Right now. Opening the document.

Maya: (skeptical) Mm-hm.

Alex: I mean it!
